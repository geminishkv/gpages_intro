And offtopic, there is another interesting case: March 2025 - tj-actions/changed-files was compromised (CVE-2025-30066): SEC-8

What happened

The attacker stole the PAT of the @tj-actions-bot bot and relinked the version tags to one malicious commit. Payload dumped the runner's memory with secrets directly into the build log - double base64

There was no external C2: the secrets were simply placed in the logs of the public repository where AWS keys, GitHub PAT, npm tokens, private RSA keys were sent

First, reviewdog/action-setup (CVE-2025-30154) was hacked, and apparently the same PAT was leaked through it. One supply-chain pulled another - SEC-3 and SEC-8 in conjunction

• SEC-8 as an entry point

• SEC-9 modified tag, where there is no link between the artifact and the code

• SEC-6 - secrets in logs and eternal creeds

• SEC-10 - noticed not by the victims, but by StepSecurity from the outside

Why didn't the pin on the tag save me from the reko?

The tags were moved to the malicious commit, and everyone who linked @v45/@v44 automatically pulled up the malware

What to do when analyzing risks? (I always say that it’s better to look at the case comprehensively and understand the technical solution itself)

• pin actions only on commit SHA - uses: owner/action@<40-hex>

• allow-list of allowed actions at the organization level

• short-lived OIDC tokens instead of static cloud keys

• after the incident - rotation of all the revealed secrets and audit of past runs (or better yet, stick everything in order in the supply-chain

Total

A real attack on the pipeline is not “breaking the application”, but stealing one PAT and rearranging the tag, then collecting secrets from everyone who trusted by name
