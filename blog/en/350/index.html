$13.7 million evaporated overnight: what the Grinex hack says about the real security of crypto exchanges

On April 15, 2026, the Grinex crypto exchange staked its stake. About 1 billion rubles were withdrawn to an unknown direction. The platform publicly blamed “Western intelligence agencies” - and did not provide a single indicator of compromise. No hash, no IP, no CVE. Nothing.

For any Threat Intelligence analyst, this is a red flag: a loud attribution without technical details means that the real vector of compromise was either not investigated, or the results are too inconvenient.

🔍 Context makes the picture even more interesting. Grinex is the actual heir of the sanctioned Garantex. After OFAC froze Garantex's assets in March 2025, client funds flowed to Grinex through the A7A5 ruble stablecoin, which processed tens of billions of dollars in a year. Sanctions pressure grew in waves - OFAC, UK, EU, blocking on Uniswap. And against this background - a cyber attack. Coincidence? Maybe. But the pattern is indicative.

⚙️ What do we know about probable techniques? If we break down the attack according to MITER ATT&CK, a typical kill chain for crypto exchanges in 2026 looks like this:

• T1078 Valid Accounts - compromise of transaction signature keys through operator or insider phishing. According to TRM Labs statistics, this is the main vector of financial cyber incidents right now

• T1190 Exploit Public-Facing Application - exchange API, especially if the code base was inherited from its predecessor without a full audit

• T1657 Financial Theft - direct theft from hot wallet, not ransomware, not encryption, but on-chain withdrawal of funds

Please note: Grinex has not published postmortem. And T1070 (clearing logs) is a standard attacker practice, which explains why IOCs never appeared.

📊 And this is just one incident against the background of a large-scale picture. In 2025, more than 767 million records of personal data of Russian users were made publicly available. Q1 2026 continued the trend - tens of millions of lines on underground forums and Telegram. Turnover fines for leaks have ceased to be a theory, and supply chain attacks have begun to hit the security tools themselves.

What should a SOC analyst do about this? At a minimum, monitor abnormal calls to signing services, track atypical transaction volumes from hot wallets, and check the integrity of CI/CD pipelines.
